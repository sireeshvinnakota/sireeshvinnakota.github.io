Recall: Induction

Proposition (Induction)

Let $S(n)$ be a statement about integers $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Suppose $S(n_0)$ is true for some $n_0 \in \mathbb{N}$, and that for all integers $k \ge n_0$, $S(k) \implies S(k+1)$. Then $S(n)$ is true for all $n \ge n_0$.

Example

Every number $10^{n+1} + 3\cdot 10^{n} + 5$ is divisible by $9$, for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

Base case ($n = 1$):

$$10^2 + 3\cdot 10 + 5 = 135 = 15 \cdot 9. \checkmark$$

Inductive step. Suppose the statement holds for $n = n_0$. Then

$$ \begin{aligned} 10^{(n_0+1)+1} + 3\cdot 10^{n_0+1} + 5 &= 10^{n_0+2} + 3\cdot 10^{n_0}\cdot 10 + (50 - 45) \\ &= 10\left(10^{n_0+1} + 3\cdot 10^{n_0} + 5\right) - 45. \end{aligned} $$

The bracketed term is divisible by $9$ by assumption, and so is $45$, so the whole expression is divisible by $9$.

The Division Algorithm

Theorem (Division Algorithm)

Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b \gt 0$. Then there exist unique $q, r \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $$a = bq + r \quad \text{with} \quad 0 \le r \lt b.$$

Why?

Construct the set

$$S = \{\, a - bk \mid k \in \mathbb{Z},\ a - bk \ge 0 \,\}.$$

If $0 \in S$, we are done, since then $b \mid a$ (take $r = 0$). If not, we will show $S \neq \emptyset$. Then by the well-ordering principle, $S$ has a least element.

Case $a \ge 0$. Then $a = a - b\cdot 0 \in S$. ✓

Case $a \lt 0$. Then $a - 2a(b) = a(1 - 2b) \in S$. ✓ (Both $a$ and $1 - 2b$ are negative, so the product is positive.)

So pick the (unique) $q$ for which the least element of $S$ is attained, and define $r = a - bq$.

Why is $r \lt b$?

Suppose not, so $r \ge b$. Then

$$a - b(q+1) = a - bq - b = r - b \ge 0,$$

so $a - b(q+1) \in S$ and is smaller than $r$. This contradicts the minimality of $r$ (that is, our choice of $q$).

We show uniqueness. Suppose not. Then there are two different pairs with $$a = bq + r \qquad \text{and} \qquad a = bq' + r'.$$

Setting these equal, $bq + r = bq' + r'$, so

$$b(q - q') = r' - r \implies b \mid r' - r \implies r' - r = 0,$$

since $|r' - r| \lt b$. Then $r = r'$, and so $q = q'$ as well, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Problems

Example

Solve $3x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$.

Step 1. Find a multiplicative inverse of $3$ modulo $7$. List some multiples of $3$:

$k$$-3$$-2$$-1$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$3k$$-9$$-6$$-3$$0$$3$$6$$9$

We see $3\cdot(-2) = -6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so the inverse of $3$ is $-2 \equiv 5 \pmod 7$.

Step 2. Multiply across by the inverse:

$$5 \cdot 3x \equiv 5 \cdot 2 \pmod 7,$$ $$x \equiv 10 \pmod 7,$$ $$x \equiv 3 \pmod 7.$$

Check: $3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$. ✓
